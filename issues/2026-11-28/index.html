A Waveform Model for the Black Holes Between Two Familiar Extremes

An analytical model follows unequal-mass black hole pairs from inspiral through ringdown, but its strongest checks lie on either side of the intermediate regime it aims to serve.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-28

How do you predict a gravitational wave when one black hole is vastly heavier than its partner, but the pair lies between the regimes best served by existing models? That awkward middle ground is the target of a preprint by Angelica Albertini and Vojtěch Witzany. Their model follows nonspinning black holes on nearly circular orbits through three stages: the inward spiral, the merger and the fading vibration of the remnant. It is designed for pairs whose heavier member exceeds the lighter one in mass by more than a factor of ten, including intermediate ratios of roughly one hundred to ten thousand.

A waveform predicts how a gravitational-wave signal changes with time. To find a weak signal in detector data, researchers need predictions that keep the rhythm of the orbit accurately over many cycles. The problem is that the principal ways to calculate that rhythm have different strengths. Numerical relativity solves Einstein’s equations on computers, but becomes costly as the masses grow more unequal. Self-force calculations expand around the limit in which one companion is much smaller than the other, but the available higher-order mass-ratio information does not settle every question in the middle. Models developed for comparable masses can also lose accuracy as the ratio rises.

Albertini and Witzany use the effective-one-body approach, which recasts a two-body problem as motion in an effective gravitational background. Their model’s dynamics are built from analytical post-Newtonian results: calculations that organize corrections to an orbital description. The authors assemble and reorganize those ingredients without tuning the new dynamics to numerical-relativity or numerical self-force data. That distinction matters here. A fit can be persuasive where reference calculations exist, yet the intermediate range is precisely where such calculations are hardest to obtain. An analytically constructed model offers a way to bridge the gap, though its behavior inside the gap still needs scrutiny.

The authors test the resulting waveforms against two kinds of reference. For mass ratios above ten and up to twenty, they report a mismatch of about one part in ten thousand to one part in one hundred thousand in the dominant wave component against numerical-relativity simulations. At ratios of ten thousand to one hundred thousand, comparisons with second-order self-force waveforms over four-year inspirals show a phase difference below one radian. These are encouraging checks at the accessible ends, not a direct validation throughout every intermediate ratio, nor a demonstration for spinning or markedly elliptical binaries. The model’s merger and ringdown coverage is useful because some proposed intermediate-ratio systems could coalesce while within a detector’s sensitive band.

Speed is part of the result. An optimized approximation to the gradual inspiral lets the authors generate waveforms as much as two orders of magnitude faster than integrating the full equations of motion. That could make repeated waveform calculations more practical when searching data or estimating source properties. Whether the same accuracy holds across the least-tested ratios, and how the construction extends beyond nonspinning, nearly circular pairs, remain open questions. The preprint supplies a candidate bridge between established calculation regimes; it does not replace the need for checks within the territory it crosses.

A Dark Matter Halo Could Change a Black Hole’s Ringing Unevenly

In a theoretical halo model, different gravitational vibrations shift by different amounts—a more revealing signature than a simple change in pitch.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-28

A disturbed black hole settles by ringing, though no sound travels through space. Its vibrations appear in gravitational waves and fade with time. If matter surrounds the black hole, does it merely lower the pitch of every vibration together, or does it alter some vibrations more than others? Kyriakos Destounis and Laura Pezzella calculate the latter possibility for a black hole inside a particular model of a dark matter halo.

Each pattern of fading vibration is called a quasinormal mode; the higher overtones are additional patterns beyond the fundamental one. The authors examine two families of gravitational disturbance, called axial and polar. For a vacuum Schwarzschild black hole, the corresponding frequencies match. In the adopted halo, surrounding matter changes the background geometry, while its own response can also couple to polar disturbances. That difference gives the two families a way to separate.

When the model halo has very low compactness, both families largely follow a familiar gravitational-redshift pattern: their frequencies change by nearly the same factor. As the halo becomes more compact, accounting for the background geometry’s horizon lapse still explains much of the change, but not all of it. A residual difference between axial and polar frequencies remains. The splitting grows from the fundamental through the third overtone, and the ratios of overtone to fundamental frequencies no longer behave like a single shared shift. The paper likens this selective change to a prism separating colors. The analogy concerns the separation of a spectrum; it does not mean dark matter is visibly refracting light.

These are calculations within a relativistic Hernquist-type halo with specified assumptions about how its matter responds, not a measurement of dark matter around a particular black hole. A different environment or matter model could produce a different pattern, and the paper does not establish that present observations can resolve the predicted differences. Its contribution is a sharper theoretical question for black-hole spectroscopy: if future data reveal unequal shifts among modes, could their pattern distinguish the influence of surrounding matter from a simple overall redshift?

Why a Quantum Black Hole May Have Fewer Ways to Fade

A fixed-energy calculation challenges an infinite classical tower of damped modes and the associated prediction of unbounded growth at an extreme horizon.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-28

A classical calculation gives a nearly extremal black hole an infinite sequence of ways to settle after a disturbance. Each successive mode fades faster. Push the sequence far enough, however, and its decay rate exceeds the black hole’s available energy above extremality. Roberto Emparan, Sameer Murthy and Stefano Trezzi take that conflict seriously: the classical decay would require black hole states outside the accessible energy spectrum.

Their method fixes the black hole’s energy and calculates a response function for a probe of specified conformal dimension. That fixed-energy choice matters because energy fluctuations become important as the hole approaches extremality; a thermal description does not select the same fixed-energy state. In the calculation, each classical mode becomes a pair of poles in the response. A pair counts as a resonant mode only when its energy lies within the accessible spectrum. Applying that condition leaves a finite collection rather than an endless tower. Below an energy threshold set by the probe and the scale of quantum throat fluctuations, no such resonances remain.

The authors also calculate what takes the tower’s place in time. For a generic probe, the response first follows one power-law decay and eventually a different one; when the energy is sufficiently high, an exponential stage can intervene. Thus removing modes does not mean the black hole stops responding. It means the classical catalogue of responses ceases to describe the entire late-time behavior. The same distinction bears on the Aretakis instability, a classical prediction that certain derivatives of a disturbance grow without bound along an extremal horizon. In this model, quantum fluctuations interrupt the classical throat behavior before that growth becomes infinite.

The result concerns an analytically controlled quantum description of the throat of a charged, nonspinning near-extremal black hole, not an observed black hole or a general calculation for every kind of horizon. Its conclusions depend on the fixed-energy setup and on the probe considered. Still, the method exposes why an apparently precise classical answer can fail: taking the semiclassical limit first and then waiting indefinitely is not equivalent to retaining quantum fluctuations at late times. How widely this altered picture of relaxation carries over to other black-hole settings remains a question.

Can a Black Hole Hide a Chaotic Collapse?

A five-dimensional construction joins a regular horizon to an interior whose geometry follows the long-sought pattern of a cosmological billiard.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-28

A black hole horizon can be smooth even if the geometry deeper inside becomes violently complicated. General relativity permits singularities, but it does not make their final approach easy to describe. One long-standing proposal says that, near a generic spacelike singularity, the shape of space repeatedly changes its pattern of collapse in a chaotic sequence. Robie A. Hennigar and Ayan K. Patra construct mathematical black holes whose interiors realize the corresponding billiard without adding matter fields to drive it.

The behavior is often pictured as a billiard. The moving point in that picture represents the changing geometry, while the walls represent constraints arising in the gravitational dynamics. Repeated encounters with the walls correspond to changes between phases of collapse. No object is literally bouncing inside the black hole: the billiard is a way to describe the equations governing the interior.

The difficult part is joining such an interior to an acceptable black hole. Earlier examples with matter fields could produce the requisite walls, while a natural four-dimensional vacuum candidate loses its chaotic behavior when conditions for a regular horizon are imposed. The authors instead construct stationary black holes in five-dimensional vacuum gravity with a negative cosmological constant. Their horizons can be squashed differently along three directions, and their exteriors are causal. They identify a condition on the horizon data needed for a chaotic interior and show that regular-horizon solutions reach an open set of billiard trajectories.

This is a theoretical construction, not evidence that an astrophysical black hole contains a chaotic singularity. Its extra spatial dimension and asymptotically locally anti-de Sitter setting differ from the usual models of observed black holes. Nor does an open set of trajectories prove that every admissible interior behaves alike. What the result establishes is narrower: the demands of a regular horizon and a causal exterior do not, in this five-dimensional vacuum setting, rule out the proposed chaotic approach to a singularity.

The people behind the paper

A Black Hole Disk Model Connects Work in Kraków and Prague

The publication trail of Paweł Doruchowski, Tomasz Grewenda, David Kofroň and Patryk Mach brings together kinetic gas, magnetic fields and Kerr geometry.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-28

What happens to a disk of charged particles when a spinning black hole and an external magnetic field both shape their motion? A preprint by Paweł Doruchowski, Tomasz Grewenda, David Kofroň and Patryk Mach approaches that question through a stationary model of a collisionless gas. The paper brings together subjects visible in earlier publications by members of the group: charged matter in a magnetic setting, gas falling toward a rotating black hole, and the geometry of a disturbed black hole.

The current paper places Doruchowski, Grewenda and Mach at Jagiellonian University in Kraków. Doruchowski lists both its School of Exact and Natural Sciences and its Institute of Theoretical Physics; Grewenda and Mach list the institute. Kofroň lists the Institute of Theoretical Physics at Charles University and the Astronomical Institute of the Czech Academy of Sciences, both in Prague. Doruchowski and Mach also appeared together on an April 2026 paper, “Relativistic figures of equilibrium in the Wald magnetosphere.” That study considered a self-gravitating, rigidly rotating charged fluid in a magnetic construction associated with stationary, axially symmetric spacetime. It treated a fluid rather than a collisionless gas, but placed charged matter, rotation, gravity and the Wald magnetosphere in the same mathematical setting.

In that earlier work, the authors showed that, in non-vacuum spacetimes, the Wald solution can be compatible with the current associated with a rigidly rotating charged fluid of vanishing electric conductivity. For fluids with constant energy density or a polytropic equation of state, they integrated the equations expressing energy-momentum conservation. They then provided numerical solutions using a modified pseudospectral code. The result was a study of equilibrium configurations in which the fluid’s own gravity was included—a different starting point from the fixed background used in the new disk paper.

Mach’s earlier publication supplies another strand. In “Bondi-type accretion onto a Kerr black hole in the kinetic regime,” published in August 2025, he derived an exact stationary solution for a Vlasov gas accreting onto a rotating black hole. Vlasov theory follows a population of particles through a distribution in position and momentum when collisions are neglected. That is also the language of the new disk calculation, although accretion and a stationary disk pose different questions. The earlier paper expressed particle current and accretion rates as explicit integrals and developed an analytic approximation for mass, energy and angular-momentum accretion rates. It used those formulas to examine characteristic timescales for black hole mass growth and spin-down under two assumptions about the surrounding energy density.

Kofroň’s publication record points toward the geometric side of the meeting. His August 2026 paper, “Reconstruction of the Weyl-Lewis-Papapetrou metric for stationary and axially symmetric gravitational perturbations of a Kerr black hole,” studied how to express disturbances of a rotating black hole’s metric in another form. It derived a gauge transformation between an averaged radiation gauge and the gauge in which the metric takes the Weyl-Lewis-Papapetrou form. Among its examples were a Schwarzschild black hole perturbed by a thin disk and a Kerr black hole perturbed by a rotating particle. The new preprint, by contrast, keeps the black hole spacetime fixed rather than calculating how its modeled gas changes that geometry.

The publication sequence is concrete: Mach’s kinetic accretion paper appeared in 2025, Doruchowski and Mach shared the charged-fluid magnetosphere paper in April 2026, and Kofroň’s Kerr-perturbation paper followed in August. The new preprint brings those authors together with Grewenda. It considers particles of the same mass and charge in a prescribed black hole spacetime and magnetic field. For thin disks around a rotating Kerr black hole, the authors obtain analytic expressions for electric current; they also study more general distribution functions on a Schwarzschild background and briefly discuss thick disks. In explicit examples the magnetic setting is the Wald magnetosphere. The calculation neglects the gas’s self-gravity and leaves the construction of quasi-neutral electron-and-ion configurations for future work.
