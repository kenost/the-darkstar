New Study Maps Curvature in Active Galaxy Jets, Probing Black Hole Dynamics

Researchers identify hundreds of active galactic nuclei with bent jets, offering new statistical insights into the complex physics of supermassive black hole systems and their outflows.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-26

Active galactic nuclei (AGNs), powered by supermassive black holes at the hearts of galaxies, often launch powerful jets of plasma that can extend for vast distances. A significant fraction of these jets appear to bend or curve on parsec scales – a few light-years from the central engine. While individual instances of such bending have been observed, the statistical properties of these curved jets and the underlying physical mechanisms driving their intricate morphologies have remained largely unconstrained. A new study, recently posted on the arXiv preprint server by V. A. Makeev, A. B. Pushkarev, and Y. Y. Kovalev, addresses this gap by undertaking a comprehensive, multi-frequency analysis of a vast dataset of AGN observations.

The team utilized an extensive collection of 123,456 Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) images, drawn from the public Astrogeo database, covering 17,478 AGNs. VLBI is a technique that combines signals from radio telescopes spread across continents, effectively creating a virtual telescope the size of Earth. This allows for incredibly high angular resolution, essential for resolving the fine structures of these distant jets on parsec scales. After carefully filtering for sources exhibiting extended structure, the researchers meticulously extracted and stacked multi-epoch, multi-frequency (ranging from 1.2 to 86 GHz) "ridge lines" – essentially the central spine of each jet. These ridge lines were then analyzed for signs of curvature.

To interpret the observed bending, Makeev, Pushkarev, and Kovalev fitted two primary physical models to the jet morphologies: linear and logarithmic helices. These helical patterns are theoretical representations of two leading mechanisms proposed to cause jet bending. The first is jet precession, where the central engine – likely the accretion disk or the black hole itself – wobbles, causing the jet to trace a conical path. The second is the resonant Kelvin–Helmholtz instability, a fluid dynamic phenomenon where shear between different layers of the jet, or between the jet and its surrounding medium, can lead to wave-like oscillations and bending. The researchers combined these model-fits with Monte Carlo simulations to further refine their understanding and constrain the parameters of these physical scenarios.

Through this rigorous analysis, the study robustly identified 586 AGNs, representing 7.4% of the sources with sufficiently resolved jets, that exhibit significant curvature on parsec scales. An interesting preliminary finding suggests that curved jets are less common at lower redshifts, a trend the authors note might be influenced by sample-related selection effects rather than an intrinsic physical property. For the precession scenario, the inferred periods for the jet's wobble spanned a wide range, from approximately 10 to 10,000 years, with a logarithmic mean period estimated at around 220 years. The authors interpret these periods as broadly consistent with solid-body disk precession in supermassive black hole binaries – systems where two supermassive black holes orbit each other. However, they caution that geodetic precession, another form of gravitational precession, would require extremely tight, near-equal-mass binary systems, making it a less universally applicable explanation. Lense–Thirring precession, which arises from the black hole's spin dragging spacetime, remains a viable mechanism for both thin and thick accretion disks. The Kelvin–Helmholtz instability also presents a compelling alternative explanation for the observed jet morphologies.

While the study provides a significant statistical leap forward, the authors emphasize that definitively identifying the preferred physical model for each individual curved jet remains an open challenge. Further investigation, including detailed temporal analysis of jet kinematics – how the jet material moves and changes over time – will be crucial to disentangling the complex interplay of forces at work. This work, currently a preprint, highlights the intricate dance between supermassive black holes, their accretion disks, and the powerful outflows they generate, underscoring the need for continued high-resolution observations and sophisticated modeling to fully unlock the secrets of these cosmic engines.

Stellar Evolution's Hidden Hand in Black Hole Mass Gap Mystery

New research explores how stellar properties like metallicity and rotation influence the maximum mass of black holes, potentially resolving a conflict with gravitational-wave observations.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-26

Why should a fusion reaction inside a star show up in a gravitational-wave catalog? During helium burning, carbon-12 can capture a helium nucleus to become oxygen-16. The rate helps set the makeup of a massive star's core. At high masses, pair instability can prevent some stars from leaving black holes, creating a predicted gap in black-hole masses. The gap's lower edge could therefore act as an indirect probe of nuclear physics—but only if the stellar-evolution model connecting the two is reliable.

GWTC-4 population analyses report an upper cutoff for low-spin black holes of 68.5 solar masses, with substantial uncertainty (+19.8 and −18.5 solar masses). Interpreted through particular stellar models, that estimate points to a carbon–oxygen reaction rate below conventional nuclear-astrophysics evaluations. Is the reaction rate the problem, or are assumptions about a star's life shifting the inferred boundary? Jiancheng Wang, Xiaogu Zhong and Jirong Mao address the latter possibility in a new arXiv preprint.

The researchers used a sophisticated stellar-evolution code called MESA to simulate the lives of massive stars. Their focus was on disentangling the complex, coupled effects of three key stellar properties: metallicity (the abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in a star), rotation (how fast a star spins), and mass-loss prescriptions (how stars shed material over their lifetimes). They found that each of these factors plays a significant role. For instance, metallicity-dependent winds, which are outflows of material from a star, strongly influence how much helium core material a star retains. Rotation, through processes like centrifugal cooling and mixing, can suppress the rate at which carbon is consumed. Crucially, different ways of modeling mass loss were found to shift the predicted edge of the black hole mass gap by 8 to 10 solar masses.

The study demonstrated that a specific combination of these factors – a relatively low metallicity, moderate rotation, and a particular type of optically-thin wind – could reproduce the maximum black hole mass inferred from the GWTC-4 gravitational-wave observations, even with the lower carbon-oxygen reaction rate. This suggests that the "canonical" 45 solar mass boundary for the mass gap might not be a universal physical threshold, but rather a statistical overlap of different black hole populations. This research provides a promising framework for jointly refining our understanding of nuclear reaction rates, the intricate physics of stellar evolution, and the populations of black holes observed through gravitational waves.

Future Gravitational-Wave Detectors Face Challenges in Unlocking Neutron Star Post-Merger Secrets

A new analysis explores the prospects for detecting the faint, high-frequency gravitational waves from the aftermath of binary neutron star collisions, highlighting the differing sensitivities to population-wide signals versus individual events.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-26

The merger of binary neutron stars (BNS) is a cataclysmic event, producing gravitational waves that current detectors can observe during the inspiral phase. However, the subsequent "post-merger" (PM) phase, occurring immediately after the stars coalesce, is particularly rich in information about the extreme physics of the merger remnant and the enigmatic equation of state of ultra-dense matter. Despite its scientific promise, the observational prospects for these PM signals, which are typically fainter and at higher frequencies, remain uncertain. A recent preprint by Jingwang Diao, Teng Zhang, and Xingjiang Zhu meticulously compares two primary strategies for detecting these elusive signals with future, more sensitive gravitational-wave observatories.

The researchers investigated two distinct detection avenues: searching for a stochastic gravitational-wave background (SGWB) generated by the collective emission from the entire population of BNS mergers across the universe, and directly detecting PM-band signals from individually resolved merger events. To ensure a consistent comparison, the study employed a unified inspiral-merger-post-merger (IPM) waveform prescription, which models the full gravitational-wave signal from the initial approach to the chaotic aftermath. This was coupled with a single, consistent astrophysical population model for BNS mergers. The team then evaluated the performance of representative third-generation gravitational-wave detector configurations, including the Einstein Telescope (ET), Cosmic Explorer (CE), and a hypothetical dedicated kilohertz detector, designed specifically for the higher frequencies expected from PM signals.

The analysis revealed a nuanced picture. For the all-source, broadband BNS SGWB, the baseline IPM model predicts that the signal would exceed the adopted detection threshold across the entire range of local merger rates considered. This suggests that future detectors, with their enhanced sensitivity, could potentially detect the background hum of countless BNS mergers. However, when focusing specifically on the kilohertz PM-band component of the SGWB, the signal is predicted to remain below this threshold. This indicates that while the overall background from BNS mergers might be detectable, isolating the specific, high-frequency signature of the post-merger phase from the collective background could prove challenging for SGWB searches.

The prospects for detecting individual PM events appear more promising, particularly with a dedicated kilohertz detector. For a local merger rate spanning 7.6 to 250 events per cubic gigaparsec per year, the baseline IPM model forecasts approximately 5 to 174 PM-band candidates per year for such a specialized instrument. This range highlights the significant impact of the assumed merger rate on detection expectations. Crucially, the study also considered cases based on numerical relativity simulations, which provide more detailed, but computationally intensive, models of the merger process. These numerical relativity cases yielded significantly lower detection rates for individual events, ranging from approximately 0.07 to 7 per year. This stark difference underscores a key limitation and uncertainty: the precise morphology and strength of post-merger gravitational waves are still subjects of active theoretical research, and different models can lead to vastly different observational predictions. The work emphasizes that while future detectors offer unprecedented opportunities, the path to unlocking the full scientific potential of BNS post-merger signals will require continued refinement of both theoretical models and detector capabilities.

The people behind the paper

How a radio team grew around the Irish LOFAR telescope

The Crab survey is the latest chapter in a collaboration that had already built data tools, tracked intermittent neutron stars and measured how plasma distorts pulsar signals.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-26

The seven names on the new Crab-pulsar preprint are not a group assembled for a single headline. Follow their earlier publications and a working collaboration comes into view: first the practical work of running a low-frequency radio station, then surveys of difficult neutron-star signals, and finally long campaigns in which the telescope itself and the plasma between stars have to be understood as carefully as the pulsar. Owen A. Johnson, David J. McKenna, Sai C. Susarla, Evan F. Keane, Letizia Vincetti, Joseph P. McCauley and Aaron Golden now bring those strands together in a six-year study of giant pulses from the Crab.

The instrument at the centre of this network is I-LOFAR at Birr Castle in Ireland, one station in a wider European radio array. Its low frequencies make it particularly good at registering the way free electrons delay and scatter radio flashes. They also make data handling and calibration indispensable. McKenna, Keane and Joe McCauley appear together on a 2023 paper about udpPacketManager, software that converts a station's raw data stream into formats researchers can analyse. The Crab paper cites that processor in its own methods. It also lists McKenna's 2024 PhD thesis at the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies and Trinity College Dublin among its references; his current affiliation on the paper is ASTRON, the Netherlands Institute for Radio Astronomy.

That software paper was not their first common scientific question. In an earlier 2023 census, McKenna, Keane and McCauley used the Irish station to follow rotating radio transients—neutron stars whose bursts arrive so sporadically that a short observation can miss them entirely. Their study covered 1,408 hours of observing and reported detections from 29 candidate sources. There is a practical continuity with the Crab campaign: both need dependable single-pulse detection across large amounts of low-frequency data, even though the source populations differ. McCauley's published work is therefore visible in both the pipeline and the observing programme, not merely in the final author list.

Johnson and Keane followed another branch of the same radio network. Alongside McKenna, they published a simultaneous two-station technosignature search in 2023. Its search target was different, but it relied on making separate LOFAR sites work together at low frequencies. Johnson, Keane and McKenna later reported radio observations of a candidate redback millisecond pulsar, a rapidly rotating neutron star interacting with a companion. Johnson's Crab-paper affiliations span Trinity College Dublin, Breakthrough Listen at Berkeley and Caltech; Keane's list Trinity. Their joint papers show a route through both radio instrumentation and compact-object science.

Susarla and Golden help connect that route to a second, longer-running problem: what happens to a pulsar's signal on its way to Earth. A 2024 study led by Susarla, with Keane and Golden among its coauthors, modelled how the changing solar wind affects pulsar timing. By 2025, Susarla, Johnson, McKenna, Keane and Golden were coauthors on an I-LOFAR study of seven pulsars near the ecliptic. It tracked arrival times and variations in dispersion measure—the amount of frequency-dependent delay caused by electrons along the line of sight—over two to three years. That five-person overlap is the clearest published bridge to the present Crab team. Susarla has since led a separate preprint on frequency-dependent changes in pulsar pulse profiles, another source of timing bias. The current paper lists Susarla at Southampton and Golden at Galway and Rhodes University in South Africa.

The same network was already studying exceptional single pulses. A 2025 preprint on RRAT-like behaviour in pulsar PSR B0656+14 carries six names from the present Crab paper: Susarla, Johnson, Vincetti, McKenna, Keane and Golden. That source is not the Crab, but its sporadic bright pulses posed a related observing problem. The overlap turns the team's history from a set of parallel interests into a sequence of shared projects.

Vincetti enters this publication trail through the telescope's response. Her 2026 station-calibration paper, coauthored by Susarla, Johnson, Keane and McKenna, used bright pulsars to test how I-LOFAR's sensitivity varies across the sky and observing band. The Crab analysis cites that work when turning received pulses into meaningful flux estimates. Vincetti's affiliations span Trinity, the Dublin Institute for Advanced Studies and Armagh Observatory. Golden's link to the Crab runs further back: a 2011 paper with him as coauthor used polarised optical light to investigate where the pulsar's emission arises. The optical work asks a different question from a radio giant-pulse survey, but it gives this team a strikingly long history with the same celestial target.

The present analysis covers 154 observing epochs, nearly 348 hours and more than a million detected single pulses. Its result depends on a chain visible in those earlier papers: capture the data, calibrate the station, distinguish intrinsic pulses from effects of intervening plasma, and keep returning to the source over years. Seen through that chain, the Crab survey is less a sudden discovery than the latest, largest product of a collaboration built around making elusive radio signals measurable.
