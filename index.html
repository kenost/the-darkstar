The lead story

Can Collapse Reach a Black Hole’s Extreme Limit?

A mathematical construction shows uncharged matter forming an exactly extremal black hole in finite time, challenging a version of black-hole thermodynamics’ third law.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-27

Can a collapsing cloud reach the exact limit that black-hole thermodynamics has long treated as unattainable in finite time? The question is sharper than whether a black hole can get very close. At the extremal limit, the black-hole and cosmological horizons of a Schwarzschild–de Sitter solution meet. A proposed third law says that a regular, continuous process cannot take a subextremal black hole all the way there in finite time while the matter obeys the weak energy condition. Mónica Tapia del Moral’s preprint constructs a mathematical counterexample using a scalar field, gravity and a positive cosmological constant—without electrical charge.

The cosmological constant matters because it changes the horizon structure. In the Schwarzschild–de Sitter family, a black-hole horizon coexists with a cosmological horizon associated with the surrounding spacetime. Extremality here concerns the limiting relation between those horizons, rather than the charge often invoked in discussions of extreme black holes. Tapia del Moral proves that certain regular, spherically symmetric initial conditions evolve into a region that is exactly extremal in finite time. The scalar field may have any nonnegative mass in the black-hole construction. The result is not a numerical collapse movie or an observation: it is an existence proof for solutions of the Einstein–scalar field equations.

The route to that proof is important. The paper uses characteristic gluing, a technique for joining suitable gravitational data along lightlike surfaces, to build initial conditions whose subsequent evolution has the required structure. The initial data are regular and have the topology of a three-sphere. At earlier times, the solution’s apparent horizon is exactly isometric to that of a subextremal Schwarzschild–de Sitter solution. An apparent horizon identifies a boundary using conditions on a particular slice of spacetime; an event horizon instead depends on the full future history of light rays. Establishing the geometry of the former does not, by itself, settle the latter.

Tapia del Moral therefore also addresses the global picture. In the constructed black-hole spacetime, the exactly extremal region is bounded toward the future by two event horizons, and the proof establishes that these are the only event horizons present. That last step is consequential: with a positive cosmological constant, configurations containing multiple black holes can exist, so additional horizons would complicate the interpretation. The paper also constructs the opposite time orientation, an extremal white hole, and a related Nariai solution when the scalar-field mass exceeds half the square root of the cosmological constant. Those extensions do not change the central black-hole finding, which does not require that extra mass condition.

The counterexample narrows what a third-law claim can mean under these assumptions. It demonstrates that regular uncharged collapse can reach exact extremality in this mathematical setting; it does not establish that an astrophysical black hole will do so. Spherical symmetry, the chosen matter model and the positive cosmological constant define the scope of the proof. The result leaves the question of which additional conditions, if any, preserve a finite-time barrier to extremality.

Could Dark Matter Cool the Gas Around M87’s Black Hole?

A proposed test treats hot infalling gas as a thermometer for light dark matter, then compares its predicted radio-to-millimeter glow with observations.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-27

The gas around a black hole is hot enough to radiate, but what if an unseen population of particles is taking some of its heat away? Ran Ding, Ningqiang Song and Cong Zhang propose using the accretion flow around M87’s supermassive black hole to test that possibility. Their target is light dark matter: hypothetical particles whose interactions are difficult to probe in conventional detectors because they can transfer so little energy to ordinary matter.

The proposed mechanism begins with an energy difference. Near the black hole, dark-matter particles with masses below roughly a million electron-volts can still carry less kinetic energy than the hot electrons in the gas, according to the paper’s model. If the two populations scatter elastically, the electrons can lose heat to the lower-energy dark matter. Electron temperature helps determine the gas’s radio-to-millimeter emission. A sufficiently strong extra cooling channel would therefore alter the spectrum astronomers receive.

The researchers added this cooling process to a model of an advection-dominated accretion flow, in which moving gas carries substantial energy inward. They solved its thermal equations for an electron-temperature profile and predicted emission, then compared that prediction with observations made alongside the 2017 Event Horizon Telescope campaign. This is a constraint from a model-data comparison, not a detection of dark matter. The comparison limits how strongly light dark matter could scatter from electrons under the assumed flow and particle models, including versions with heavy or light mediators—the particles that would carry the interaction.

The method asks whether dark matter would disturb an observable heat balance, even if individual dark-matter particles leave no direct signal. The observed spectrum must also be interpreted through the physics of accreting gas and its other emission components, including a jet. The preprint’s limits are consequently conditional on that modeling. It offers a complementary route to the dark-matter question: instead of trying to catch a light particle’s tiny recoil in a laboratory, look for the heat it might remove from a black hole’s surroundings.

How Tall Is a Neutron Star’s Accretion Shock?

Four relativistic simulations offer geometric estimates for intensely fed neutron stars, but translating a modeled column into an X-ray spectral fit remains an interpretive step.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-27

A magnetic neutron star can funnel incoming matter toward its poles, where radiation pressure may slow the flow before it strikes the surface. The height of that slowdown and the width of the resulting accretion column affect how X-rays escape. Neither dimension is simply photographed. Fatemeh Kayanikhoo and Marios Kouzis instead use four axisymmetric simulations of super-Eddington accretion to estimate the column’s geometry and compare it with quantities inferred from X-ray spectra.

Their calculations combine general relativity, radiation, magnetic fields and flowing gas. The simulated neutron stars have maximum surface magnetic fields spanning 30 billion to 100 billion gauss. The authors measure the angular width of each column and estimate a characteristic radiation-shock height from the flow speed and how rapidly that speed changes. Across the four models, the width decreases from about 0.14 to 0.07 radians while the estimated height increases from 1.95 to 3.89 kilometres. Those are properties of the modeled flows, not direct measurements of a particular neutron star.

The comparison with observations requires another translation. X-ray spectral models infer dimensions through assumptions about the gas and how photons scatter and escape. Kayanikhoo and Kouzis calculate an area-equivalent radius for each simulated column—a single radius representing its area—so that the simulation can be set beside values from the spectral model called bwcycl. Their radii range from 5.2 to 3.2 kilometres; three lie in the upper part of the range inferred from those spectral fits. Agreement at that level does not establish that the simulated columns reproduce every feature of the observed systems.

The method also defines the limits of the finding. Four models sample a restricted range of magnetic fields, and axisymmetry excludes structures that vary around the star. Shock heights are estimated from velocity behaviour rather than measured as an observed boundary. The spectral comparison likewise depends on its own model of how radiation emerges. The work forms a bridge between two ways of describing the same difficult region: a simulated flow with explicit geometry and an X-ray spectrum whose shape carries indirect clues to that geometry.

Do Waveform Models Invent Clues to Black Hole Origins?

A simulation study finds that model choice can shift some individual measurements, while population-level spin–mass patterns remain broadly recoverable at current design sensitivity.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-27

The gravitational wave from two merging black holes does not arrive with a label saying how the pair formed. Researchers instead look for patterns in properties inferred from many mergers. Two useful clues are mass ratio—how unequal the black holes are—and effective spin, a measure of how their spins line up with the pair’s orbit. Different formation routes may populate different parts of that two-property map. But the properties affect the wave in related ways, making them hard to disentangle. Could the mathematical waveform used to interpret a signal create a misleading pattern?

Upasana Das and Suvodip Mukherjee tested that question with an injection-and-recovery study. They generated simulated signals using one waveform model, NRSur7dq4, as the reference and analyzed them with two others, SEOBNRv5PHM and IMRPhenomTPHM. The deliberate mismatch reveals what happens when the analysis model differs from the signal model. It is a test of modeling error under specified conditions, not proof that any one model perfectly describes nature.

The preprint finds regions—particularly among unequal-mass binaries—where waveform choice can noticeably affect recovered properties. Some catalogued events, including GW190403_051519 and GW240527_230910, lie near regions the authors identify as more susceptible. That does not mean those events have been shown to be wrongly measured. At the population level, however, the authors find that waveform systematics are unlikely to manufacture a spin–mass-ratio correlation or prevent recovery of the underlying population properties for current detectors at their design sensitivity.

The distinction between one event and a population is central. A model mismatch may make an individual merger look unusual without producing the same misleading trend across many mergers. The result supports using spin–mass patterns to investigate formation channels under the study’s tested conditions, while keeping greater caution around susceptible individual events. It does not identify a definitive formation route for the observed black holes.

The people behind the paper

The Researchers Behind a Faster Gravitational-Wave Alert

A far-flung team’s publication trail runs from pulsar searches and sky-localization methods to the practical challenge of telling other astronomers where—and what—to look for.

THE DARKSTAR SCIENCE DESK · 2026-11-27

When a gravitational-wave candidate appears, a quick message can help other astronomers decide where and what to search. A preprint on alerts from LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA’s fourth observing run describes public information about chirp mass and possible sub-solar-mass objects, alongside tests of the delivery infrastructure. Sushant Sharma Chaudhary and Andrew Toivonen are marked as equal contributors. The wider team’s publication record traces several lines of work that meet around the alert: searches for faint signals, tests of gravity, sky localization and follow-up astronomy.

The paper lists Chaudhary at the University of Minnesota and Toivonen at Minnesota and Los Alamos National Laboratory. Deep Chatterjee is listed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Roberto De Pietri at institutions in Parma, Alvin K. Y. Li at the University of Tokyo, Cody Messick and Brandon Piotrzkowski at the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, and Geoffrey Mo at MIT and institutions in Pasadena. Their shared publications record overlapping collaborations, while individual papers show the variety of questions represented on the author list.

One thread reaches back to a 2019 narrow-band search for gravitational waves from known pulsars. Chatterjee, De Pietri, Messick and Mo were among the current paper’s authors who also appeared on that work. It searched data from 33 pulsars for the nearly continuous signal an asymmetric spinning neutron star might emit, allowing a small mismatch between the assumed and true signal evolution. No signal was found, and the authors reported upper limits. A later shared publication examined gravitational waves associated with the 2024 Vela pulsar glitch. It found no significant detection candidates but set direct strain limits stricter than those indirectly inferred from the glitch’s overall energy scale.

Another shared search, using the first eight months of the fourth observing run, targeted continuous waves from 15 nearby supernova remnants likely to host young neutron stars. It used five pipelines and found no evidence of a signal. The researchers set limits on strain amplitude, including their strongest constraint near 300 hertz for Vela Jr. These projects address signals different from merger alerts, but they document repeated collaboration on extracting constraints from detector data even when a search produces no detection.

Other work leads toward the decisions that follow a candidate. In a 2021 paper, Piotrzkowski and coauthors developed a joint ranking statistic to assess whether gravitational-wave and other astronomical candidates fit together better than random coincidences. They incorporated shared parameters and galaxy catalogues, and tested the methods on known and tentative joint candidates. Li coauthored a 2026 simulation study asking how combining repeated images of a strongly lensed gravitational-wave source could improve its sky localization. The largest typical gain came from combining two images, reducing the 90% credible sky area by an order of magnitude.

Michael W. Coughlin, listed at Minnesota in the new paper, coauthored a study of whether kilonova light curves can help recover a neutron-star merger’s time under different observing conditions. De Pietri coauthored a 2023 study of the prospects for recovering inertial modes in long-lived neutron-star merger remnants. It injected numerical post-merger waveforms into simulated noise and used BayesWave to reconstruct them. Mo coauthored a 2026 paper presenting a photon-counting method for fast, low-noise astronomical imagers, demonstrated on optical data that included pulsar detections. These publications span timing, dense-matter signals and faint light; they do not assign their authors particular roles in the alert paper.

A documented bridge to the new work is a search for mergers with sub-solar-mass components in data from the second part of the fourth observing run. Published in October 2026, it shares numerous authors with the alert preprint, including Chaudhary, Chatterjee, De Pietri, Li, Messick, Mo and Coughlin. Two of three search pipelines reported outlying candidates, but none at sufficient significance to establish a detection; the study then placed limits on possible populations. The new paper describes HasSSM, a public product estimating the probability that a candidate contains a sub-solar-mass object. Search limits and low-latency alerts answer different questions, but the shared authors and subject show an earlier collaboration around that class of source.

The team’s shared record also includes tests of general relativity using merger signals from the fourth gravitational-wave transient catalog. One paper examined merger remnants and found overall consistency with relativity, with no strong evidence for a deviation. In the new work, the collaboration turns to the infrastructure itself: a simulated-signal challenge and software for replaying recorded triggers through downstream processing. The paper reports that some products performed less well on the challenge’s particular simulated population. Across these projects, overlapping groups have worked on searches, limits and interpretation as well as the practical systems that make candidate information available to other observers.
